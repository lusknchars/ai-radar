AgentDrift: A Step-Labeled Benchmark of Injection-Hijacked LLM Agent Trajectories

An agent can follow an instruction hidden in a document instead of its user's request. AgentDrift provides labeled synthetic examples for studying where that happens. Its most useful lesson for a builder is how an impressive evaluation score can come from recognizing the test data.

For solo builders

Paperraft interpretation of the full paper. The product example and test below are proposals.

Possible product use: an assistant that reads customer uploads or fetched web pages before calling your APIs.

What you would change

AgentDrift labels individual steps of synthetic tool-use histories. It supplies evaluation data, not a proven protective layer. Some examples contain attacks; others contain suspicious-looking but legitimate content.

Try the idea on your product

A proposed first test. Set your budget and success criteria before running it.

Use the dataset to challenge your existing checks offline. Keep tools mocked so that a test cannot send messages, alter records, or expose real customer data.

Compare against

Your current detector against a small human-reviewed set. Separate real attack success, resisted attacks, and legitimate content that looks suspicious.

  1. Fetch the official dataset linked by the paper, record its version and license, and inspect the label definitions. Start with a small sample of attacked and legitimate histories that resemble your workflow.
  2. Split by world identity so the same world cannot appear in training and evaluation. Keep product test examples separate from any detector tuning. Check for repeated templates and names.
  3. Replay the held-out cases through your existing checks with mocked tools. Record the exact step flagged, missed successful attacks, and legitimate actions blocked.
  4. Only then compare a candidate detection change on the same cases. Manually review disagreements and repeat with held-out examples from your own agent before enabling enforcement.

What to measure

Attack recall, false-positive rate on legitimate tasks, delay until detection, review burden, and detector cost. Keep denominators and attack types visible instead of reporting one overall score.

When to keep it

Keep a change only if it catches more consequential attacks within your preselected false-alarm and latency limits. Synthetic benchmark performance alone is not evidence that a production agent is protected.

Risk notes

Conditions that may negate the gain or block adoption.

  1. risk 01Paperraft inference

    All 12,536 trajectories come from a single generator model, so a detector may learn the generator's stylistic fingerprint rather than injection semantics; transfer to production agent traces is untested.

  2. risk 02Paperraft inference

    World-identity leakage in four domains means results on the default split without anonymization or world-held-out splitting are not evidence of injection detection.

  3. risk 03Paperraft inference

    Template concentration (28.9% of injections naming one fictitious domain; ~75% of hard negatives sharing three motifs) permits memorization unless target domains and amounts are masked at test time.

  4. risk 04Paperraft inference

    Injections almost always fall at step 2 or 3 under fixed positional templates, enabling positional-prior shortcuts.

  5. risk 05Paperraft inference

    Labels are protocol-specified and structurally validated rather than independently human-annotated; the 99.6% correctness figure is an estimate from a 1,200-trajectory sample.

  6. risk 06Paperraft inference

    Short trajectories (3–11 steps), single agents, and no real agent traffic limit applicability to long-horizon or multi-agent settings.

Minimum useful test

The smallest test intended to disprove the technique on a local workload.

  1. Download the released corpus (CC BY 4.0, GitHub) and verify the counts and grammar claims directly: 12,536 trajectories, per-category label strings matching the stated regular languages, and the 70/15/15 split.
  2. Reproduce the world-identity lookup check (Table XI) on the test split; if a world-lookup predictor does not reach ~86% binary accuracy on your copy, the leakage claim fails; if it does, apply world anonymization or a world-held-out split before any further evaluation.
  3. Re-implement the six-feature logistic regression baseline (specified in Section IX-A, reproducible 'with a few lines of code') and confirm it recovers ~55% of attacks overall but under 25% on partial hijacks and delayed executions; if simple features solve those patterns, the paper's core difficulty claim fails.
  4. Train a small sequence model (e.g., a short transformer or BiLSTM over the documented step linearization) on CPU or a single small GPU under the world-anonymized protocol; if it does not beat the surface baseline on partial-hijack and delayed-execution recall, the claimed need for sequence modeling is not demonstrated on your workload.
  5. Spot-audit a random sample of attacked and hard-negative trajectories by hand against the category definitions to check whether the 99.6% label-correctness claim holds for your use case.

Questions before adoption

What still needs reading or measurement.

  • Does a sequence-aware detector trained on AgentDrift actually outperform the surface baseline under the world-anonymized and world-held-out protocols, and by how much on partial hijack and delayed execution?
  • Does training on this synthetic distribution transfer to real agent traces or to trajectories from other generators?
  • What inter-annotator agreement would human labeling of the injection index and hijacked span yield (listed as future work)?
  • Are the released GitHub artifacts (data files, tool pools, loader) complete and consistent with the paper's reported counts?
  • What compute budget is needed to train a competitive sequence model on the corpus (not reported; only the trivial baseline is evaluated)?
