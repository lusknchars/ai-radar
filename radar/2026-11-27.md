#### 1. Periodic state reset in multi-agent search

The method replaces continuous long-horizon agent search (single-agent iteration, parallel agents, or communicating teams that accumulate full history) with periodic restarts: all agent sessions and candidate history are discarded and the search resumes from the best candidate found so far. It costs no extra compute or memory relative to the baseline strategies, since it operates at fixed budget, but adds orchestration logic for checkpointing best candidates, scheduling resets, and re-seeding agents; reset frequency becomes a new hyperparameter. It can fail when the discarded history contained information needed to escape the current basin, when the best-so-far candidate is a local optimum that restarts cannot leave, or when task verifiers are noisy enough that the retained candidate is misjudged; the reported gains are also limited to four specific open-ended tasks and may not transfer.

#### 2. Transparent deterministic context compaction with verbatim prose retention

It replaces model-written summary compaction (and rewording-based deterministic compaction) with a deterministic scheme that keeps user and assistant prose verbatim and explicitly flags where information was stripped. Cost is low in complexity and compute, but retaining verbatim text preserves less aggressively than summarization, so effective context capacity may be tighter and no extra latency figures are given. It can fail when verbatim retention crowds out needed context under heavy compaction, and the evidence rests on the authors' own benchmarks tied to the Pi coding agent, so transfer to other agents and workloads is unverified.

#### 3. Action-level on-policy distillation for agents

ActionOPD replaces offline imitation and token-level on-policy distillation with action-level on-policy distillation, rewarding student actions by semantic agreement with sampled teacher actions, so teacher token probabilities and shared tokenization are no longer required. It costs an on-policy RL training loop with sampling from both student and teacher at each state, which is substantially more compute- and orchestration-intensive than offline supervised fine-tuning, though the teacher can be a black-box API. It can fail if the sampled teacher actions are unrepresentative or the semantic-agreement reward is noisy, and the gains are demonstrated only on curated tool-use tasks, so transfer to other agent workloads is unverified.
